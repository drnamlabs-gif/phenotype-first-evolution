\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Can organisms write what they learn into heredity? Denis Noble proposed that
organisms possess read--write access to an inheritable epigenome, rather than
only influencing evolution indirectly through selection. We give this
hypothesis its first direct computational test, pairing simulation with an
exact analytical account. In \Pheno{}, organisms carry two heritable layers:
a genetic layer, inherited with mutation, and an epigenetic layer of learned
patterns, inherited unchanged. Against a Baldwin-Effect control with
identical learning but no inheritance of what is learned, \Pheno{} performs
identically on simple one-pattern tasks, but achieves 3--6$\times$ higher
fitness on compositional two-pattern tasks ($p < 0.001$, Cohen's $d =
3.44$--$4.89$), including one task reproduced identically across all 30 runs
(s.d.\ $= 0.0$). On a third compositional task both mechanisms fail equally.
Because the model's phenotype space is finite, we then explain every one of
these outcomes exactly, by enumeration. Baldwin learning here has zero
heritability, so its expected response to selection is zero; its observed
fitness matches the predicted neutral-drift value (0.086 predicted and
observed on T+Plus). Epigenomic write-back acts as a ratchet whose fitness
can only rise, converging to a ceiling set by what plasticity can reach: the
predicted ceiling 0.517 equals the observed plateau exactly, and the
perfect reproducibility follows because every run reaches the same algebraic
ceiling. The equal-failure case arises because no reachable phenotype clears
the write-back threshold, which switches the ratchet off. Extended agency
thus pays precisely when acquired solutions are heritable, accumulable, and
reachable.

\vspace{0.8em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} extended evolutionary synthesis; epigenetic inheritance;
organismal agency; Baldwin Effect; computational evolution; compositionality;
direct write-back.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many complex problems require solving multiple interconnected challenges
sequentially. In biology, protein structures are not random amino acid sequences
but composed hierarchies: hydrophobic cores, signal peptides, binding domains,
regulatory regions -- each layer building on previous ones. Metabolic pathways
are not isolated reactions but networks in which each enzyme depends on prior
enzymatic solutions. Major evolutionary transitions -- the origin of eukaryotes,
the evolution of multicellularity, the emergence of nervous systems -- all
involve layered innovations where each layer builds on those before it
\citep{MaynardSmith1995}.

The central challenge is solving these compositional problems efficiently. If
each generation must rediscover all prior solutions before learning new ones,
the time required grows exponentially. But if organisms could inherit
accumulated solutions from their parents -- not just genes, but learned knowledge
encoded in heritable form -- offspring could build upon parental achievements
rather than start from scratch. The fundamental question is whether organisms
can inherit accumulated solutions, enabling learning to accumulate across
generations.

For 150 years, evolutionary biology understood inheritance narrowly: genes pass
from parent to offspring, variation arises through mutation, and selection
determines which alleles persist \citep{Darwin1859,Huxley1942}. Darwin's theory
and the Modern Synthesis placed genetic inheritance at evolution's center. The
Extended Evolutionary Synthesis (EES) recognised that organisms actively shape
evolution through niche construction, developmental plasticity, and learning
\citep{LalandEtAl2015,Uller2015,WestEberhard2003}. However, EES mechanisms
remain \emph{indirect}: organisms learn during their lifetime, this influences
selection pressure, and only across many subsequent generations do genes slowly
encode learned traits through genetic assimilation
\citep{Waddington1942,HintonNowlan1987}. Biologically, the epigenome -- DNA
methylation, histone modifications, chromatin marks -- provides an alternative
inheritance channel. These marks persist through meiosis and respond to
environmental signals, enabling transgenerational inheritance of acquired
information independent of genetic change
\citep{JablonkaLamb2005,Pembrey2006,WaterlandJirtle2007,Jablonka2014}.

It remains unknown whether organisms can write learned knowledge directly into
an inheritable epigenome, and whether such inheritance provides an evolutionary
advantage over selection-only learning. To test this, we implement
\textbf{Phenotype-First} (the \Pheno{} algorithm), a computational model in
which organisms maintain two independent heritable layers: a genetic layer
(a bit-string genome, inherited with mutation) and an epigenetic layer (a list
of learned patterns, inherited unchanged and in full). During learning,
organisms add to the epigenetic layer; offspring inherit both layers, the
genetic one with mutation and the epigenetic one unchanged.

A critical distinction separates \Pheno{} from classical Lamarckism. Lamarck
proposed write-back to the genes themselves, which fails because somatic
modifications cannot alter germline DNA. Our approach targets \emph{epigenomic}
inheritance -- the regulatory layer controlling gene expression, not the genes
themselves -- which is biologically documented in systems ranging from
environmental stress responses to transgenerational disease susceptibility
\citep{Pembrey2006,Rakyan2002}.

We test \Pheno{} against the \emph{Baldwin Effect} (organisms learn during
their lifetime, which influences which organisms reproduce, but learned
knowledge dies with the organism) \citep{Baldwin1896}, with a learning-free
\emph{Gene-Centric} baseline establishing that learning is necessary at all.
We make three predictions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item On simple learning tasks (learn one pattern), the two learning
        mechanisms perform equivalently -- inheritance offers no advantage when
        problems are solved within a single lifetime.
  \item On tasks requiring the simultaneous expression of multiple learned
        patterns, \Pheno{} achieves several-fold higher fitness than the
        Baldwin Effect -- inherited learning lets offspring build on parental
        knowledge instead of rediscovering prior solutions.
  \item On tasks where no reachable learned state clears the write-back
        threshold, the two mechanisms fail equally -- revealing inherent limits
        to inheritance-based learning.
\end{enumerate}

We test these predictions in simulation (30 independent replicates per
condition) and then go further: because the model's phenotype space is finite
and enumerable, we derive an exact analytical account that reproduces every
observed outcome -- including the precise value of a fitness plateau, its zero
variance across runs, and the equal-failure case -- from first principles.

\section{Background}

The Modern Synthesis placed genes at the centre of evolution: genes are the only
heritable material, mutations create variation, and selection determines which
alleles spread \citep{Darwin1859,Huxley1942}. In this framework organisms are
passive carriers of genes -- they respond to selection pressure but do not direct
their own evolution. Evolution is one-way: genes shape phenotypes, phenotypes
compete under selection, successful alleles persist.

The Extended Evolutionary Synthesis challenged this gene-centric view by
recognising that organisms actively shape their evolution
\citep{LalandEtAl2015,Uller2015,WestEberhard2003}. Niche construction allows
organisms to modify their environment, influencing which phenotypes are adaptive
\citep{OdlingSmee2003}. Developmental plasticity permits rapid phenotypic change
without genetic modification \citep{WestEberhard2003,FuscoMinelli2010}. Learning
influences selection: organisms acquiring useful behaviours survive better, and
over generations selection favours genotypes supporting such learning -- the
Baldwin Effect \citep{Baldwin1896,HintonNowlan1987}. Yet despite recognising
organismal agency, EES mechanisms remain indirect: learning influences evolution
only through its effect on selection pressure, and knowledge accumulation is
slow, requiring genetic assimilation across many generations
\citep{Waddington1942,Waddington1956}.

Denis Noble proposed something more radical: organisms possess not just agency
\emph{within} evolution but agency \emph{over heredity itself}
\citep{Noble2016,Noble2017,Noble2021}. To borrow his metaphor from \emph{The
Music of Life}: organisms are not merely musicians performing a fixed genetic
score written by natural selection -- they are composers. They read the genetic
score, yes, but they also compose new notes (learned patterns) and write them
directly into the hereditary sheet music (the epigenome). Critically, they pass
this newly composed music to offspring without waiting for slow genetic
assimilation. Evolution becomes bidirectional: organisms actively write new
information into the heredity they pass forward.

Epigenomic inheritance is biologically real. DNA methylation patterns, histone
modifications, and chromatin marks persist through meiosis and respond to
environmental signals, enabling transgenerational inheritance of acquired
information independent of genetic sequence change
\citep{Pembrey2006,Rakyan2002,WaterlandJirtle2007,Gibney2010,Jablonka2014}. The
biological mechanism clearly exists. Yet Noble's hypothesis -- that organisms can
directly write learned patterns to this heritable layer -- has never been tested
computationally. Existing computational work tests the Baldwin Effect
\citep{Baldwin1896,HintonNowlan1987,Suzuki2007} and genetic assimilation, but
none directly tests whether organisms can immediately write learned patterns to
heritable epigenomic form, or whether this accelerates learning. We implement
\Pheno{} to close that gap.

\section{Methods}

We report two experiments on $10 \times 10$ binary grids
(Figure~\ref{fig:task}). Experiment~1 establishes, on simple single-pattern
tasks, that lifetime learning is necessary and that the inheritance mechanism
is irrelevant when one lifetime suffices. Experiment~2 is the core test: on
compositional two-pattern tasks, it compares learning \emph{without}
inheritance (Baldwin) against learning \emph{with} epigenomic write-back
(\Pheno{}), holding the learning operator itself identical. The two
experiments use different encodings and fitness functions, each described in
full below; all quantitative claims in this paper are tied to the experiment
they come from.

\subsection{Experiment 1: simple tasks}
\label{sec:exp1}

\paragraph{Targets and fitness.}
Three target patterns are defined, each occupying 5 fixed cells: \textbf{L}
(a 3-cell vertical arm meeting a 3-cell horizontal arm at a shared corner),
\textbf{T} (a 3-cell horizontal bar with a 2-cell stem), and \textbf{Plus} (a
5-cell cross). Fitness is translation-invariant Intersection-over-Union: the
organism's cell set is compared against the target at every offset within a
$\pm5$-cell window and the best alignment is kept,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Fitness} \;=\; \max_{(\Delta x,\Delta y)\,\in\,[-5,5]^2}
\frac{|\,P_{\Delta x,\Delta y} \cap T\,|}{|\,P_{\Delta x,\Delta y} \cup T\,|},
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the set of cells the organism occupies, $T$ the target, and
$P_{\Delta x,\Delta y}$ the organism's cell set shifted by
$(\Delta x,\Delta y)$. IoU penalises cells occupied outside the target, so
extra activity is not free.

\paragraph{Encoding and algorithms.}
Each organism carries a 100-bit genome parsed as twenty 5-bit instructions;
each instruction selects a drawing primitive from a 9-primitive library (two
lines, four corner pieces, three junctions) and a stamp position, and the
genetically-coded phenotype is the union of all stamped primitives. Three
algorithms are compared. \emph{Gene-Centric}: no learning; asexual
reproduction with tournament selection (size 3), 10\% elitism, and 2\%
per-bit mutation. \emph{Baldwin Effect}: identical, plus 20 learning trials
per lifetime; each trial composes 1--4 randomly chosen primitives at random
positions and replaces the phenotype if fitness improves; nothing learned is
inherited. \emph{\Pheno{}}: identical to Baldwin, except that learned
configurations scoring above 0.7 are appended to a heritable pattern list,
offspring inherit that list unmutated, and an organism may adopt the
best-scoring pairwise union of its inherited patterns.

\subsection{Experiment 2: compositional tasks}
\label{sec:exp2}

\paragraph{Targets and fitness.}
Three fixed-position target patterns are defined
(Figure~\ref{fig:task}E): \textbf{L} (5 cells, upper-left region), \textbf{T}
(6 cells, upper-right region), and \textbf{Plus} (5 cells, grid centre). A
compositional task pairs two of them (L+T, T+Plus, or L+Plus), and fitness is
the mean of the two per-pattern recalls computed at the patterns' fixed
positions,
\begin{equation}
F(P) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\left(
\frac{|P \cap T_1|}{|T_1|} \;+\; \frac{|P \cap T_2|}{|T_2|} \right),
\label{eq:comp-fitness}
\end{equation}
where $T_1, T_2$ are the two component patterns. There is no translation
search and no penalty for extra cells: fitness is monotone non-decreasing in
the organism's cell set, a property that the analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:theory} exploits.

\paragraph{Generative encoding.}
Each organism carries a 100-bit genome divided into five 20-bit blocks, one
per shape in a 5-shape library (an L-piece, a T-piece, a cross, a 5-cell
line, and a 5-cell block). Within each block only the first four bits are
read (the remainder is non-coding): one activation bit, one bit selecting
horizontal offset $\in \{0, 5\}$, one bit selecting vertical offset
$\in \{0, 5\}$, and one bit selecting scale $\in \{1, 3\}$ (scaled shapes
clip at the grid boundary). A block is therefore in one of 9 states (off, or
one of $2 \times 2 \times 2$ placements), and the phenotype is the union of
the placed shapes. The full phenotype space has at most $9^5 = 59{,}049$
elements -- small enough to enumerate exactly, which
Section~\ref{sec:theory} does.

\paragraph{Learning.}
Both algorithms use the identical learning operator: in each of 20 trials per
lifetime, a fresh genome is drawn uniformly at random, decoded, and (for
\Pheno{}) unioned with the organism's inherited patterns; the organism's
fitness is the best value found across its trials. Learning is thus
deliberately \emph{unconstrained by the organism's own genome}: every
organism explores the same phenotype distribution, so the two algorithms
differ in inheritance only. This isolates the variable under test -- what
happens to acquired information at reproduction -- from any genetic guidance
of learning.

\paragraph{Inheritance and selection.}
\emph{Baldwin}: offspring receive the parent's genome with 2\% per-bit
mutation and nothing else; each generation learns from scratch.
\emph{\Pheno{}}: offspring additionally receive, unmutated and in full, the
parent's accumulated pattern list, to which any pattern the parent learned
this lifetime is appended if its fitness exceeded the write-back threshold
$\theta = 0.5$. Selection in both: the population of 50 is sorted by learned
fitness, the top 10 survive unchanged (elitism), and the remaining 40 slots
are filled by offspring of parents drawn uniformly from the top 25. Runs
last 150 generations. The reported final fitness is that of the phenotype an
offspring would inherit: the genome-decoded phenotype (Baldwin) or its union
with the accumulated pattern list (\Pheno{}).

\subsection{Experimental design and statistical analysis}

Experiment~1 runs three algorithms on three simple tasks; Experiment~2 runs
Baldwin and \Pheno{} on three compositional tasks. Every task--algorithm
combination is replicated 30 times with independent random seeds (540 runs in
total), with populations of 50 evolving for 150 generations. We record final
fitness, per-component recall (compositional tasks), and convergence time.
For each task we apply one-way ANOVA across algorithms with Bonferroni
correction ($\alpha = 0.05$), checking assumptions by Shapiro--Wilk and
Levene's tests and substituting Welch's $F$ where violated; pairwise Baldwin
vs.\ \Pheno{} comparisons use Welch's $t$-test with Cohen's $d$. All
algorithms are implemented in Python; the complete code, raw per-run results,
and the exact-enumeration analysis script of Section~\ref{sec:theory} are
publicly available (see Data and code availability).

\section{An exact analytical account}
\label{sec:theory}

The compositional environment of Experiment~2 is simple enough to be solved
in closed form: the genome has $9^5 = 59{,}049$ distinct decodings, the
learning operator draws from a fixed, fully known distribution over them, and
the fitness function~(\ref{eq:comp-fitness}) is monotone in the organism's
cell set. We exploit these three facts to derive, by exact enumeration (no
mean-field or other approximation), the expected outcome of every condition.
Throughout, $X$ denotes a uniformly random genome, $F(\cdot)$ the task
fitness~(\ref{eq:comp-fitness}), and $\theta = 0.5$ the write-back
threshold. Proofs are short and sketched inline; the enumeration script is
published with the code.

\subsection{Reachability ceilings}

Let $R$ be the union of all cells reachable by any shape placement of the
encoding (Figure~\ref{fig:task}F). No phenotype -- and no union of phenotypes,
however many generations accumulate -- can occupy a cell outside $R$.

\begin{proposition}[Ceiling]\label{prop:ceiling}
For every phenotype $P$ expressible by the encoding, and every union of such
phenotypes, $F(P) \le F^* := \tfrac12\bigl(|R \cap T_1|/|T_1| + |R \cap
T_2|/|T_2|\bigr)$. Enumeration gives per-pattern ceilings
$\mathrm{recall}_{\max}(\mathrm{L}) = 4/5$,
$\mathrm{recall}_{\max}(\mathrm{T}) = 5/6$,
$\mathrm{recall}_{\max}(\mathrm{Plus}) = 1/5$, hence
$F^*(\mathrm{L{+}T}) = 0.817$, $F^*(\mathrm{T{+}Plus}) = 0.517$,
$F^*(\mathrm{L{+}Plus}) = 0.500$.
\end{proposition}

The Plus target sits at the grid centre, and the encoding's offsets
$\{0,5\}$ reach only one of its five cells: the Plus pattern is almost
entirely outside what plasticity can express. This single geometric fact
drives everything that follows.

\subsection{Baldwin: zero heritability, zero response}

In Experiment~2 the learning operator draws fresh random genomes, so an
organism's learned fitness is the maximum of 20 i.i.d.\ draws of $F(X)$ --
independent of its own genome and of its ancestry.

\begin{proposition}[Null response]\label{prop:baldwin}
Under the Baldwin Effect, every organism's learned fitness in every
generation is an independent copy of $Y = \max\{F(X_1),\dots,F(X_{20})\}$.
Fitness is therefore completely non-heritable; selection has nothing
heritable to act on, and the expected population-best learned fitness is
constant over generations at $\mathbb{E}[\max$ of $1000$ draws$]$
($0.747$, $0.453$, $0.486$ for L+T, T+Plus, L+Plus). Moreover, because
selection is independent of genomes and bit-flip mutation is symmetric, the
marginal distribution of every genome remains exactly uniform in every
generation; the recorded final fitness (the genome-decoded phenotype of the
best learner) is therefore a draw of $F(X)$, with expectation
$\mathbb{E}[F(X)] = 0.204$, $0.086$, $0.130$ respectively.
\end{proposition}

This is the Baldwin Effect's failure mode laid bare: learning raises the
fitness used by selection, but with zero heritability the breeder's equation
returns zero response, and the heritable line drifts neutrally. The observed
Baldwin means ($0.254$, $0.086$, $0.177$) match the neutral-drift
predictions within sampling error -- exactly, to three decimals, on T+Plus.

\subsection{Phenopoiesis: the write-back ratchet}

\begin{proposition}[Ratchet]\label{prop:ratchet}
Along any \Pheno{} lineage, the inherited union $I_t$ of accumulated patterns
is non-decreasing under set inclusion, and since $F$ is monotone,
$F(I_t)$ is non-decreasing in $t$ and bounded by $F^*$. Once any stored
pattern clears $\theta$, elitist selection preserves the lineage and
subsequent stored patterns (each necessarily scoring $\ge F(I_t)$) keep
enlarging $I_t$; $F(I_t)$ then converges to $F^*$. Engagement is fast
wherever $p := \Pr[F(X) > \theta] > 0$: with $1000$ draws per generation the
per-generation engagement probability is $1 - (1-p)^{1000}$ -- effectively
$1$ for L+T ($p = 0.049$) and $0.24$ for T+Plus ($p = 2.7 \times 10^{-4}$),
i.e.\ expected engagement within $\sim$4 generations.
\end{proposition}

This resolves both of the simulation's striking observations at once. The
T+Plus plateau equals $F^* = (5/6 + 1/5)/2 = 0.5167$ exactly, and its
variance is zero because every run climbs the same ratchet to the same
algebraic ceiling: what looked like ``perfect reproducibility of learning''
is deterministic convergence to a reachability bound. On L+T, runs approach
but do not all exhaust the ceiling within 150 generations
($0.772 \pm 0.075$ vs.\ $F^* = 0.817$), because the final increments require
rare draws covering the last unclaimed target cells.

\subsection{The write threshold and the hard task}

\begin{proposition}[Knife edge]\label{prop:knife}
For L+Plus, enumeration of all $59{,}049$ phenotypes gives
$\max_P F(P) = 0.500$ exactly. Since write-back requires $F > \theta = 0.5$
strictly, no pattern is ever stored: the epigenome stays empty and \Pheno{}
reduces to the Baldwin Effect identically.
\end{proposition}

The predicted consequence -- statistical indistinguishability of the two
mechanisms on L+Plus -- is what the simulation shows ($p = 0.789$), with both
observed means at the neutral-drift level of
Proposition~\ref{prop:baldwin}. We stress the interpretation: the two target
patterns do not overlap on the grid; the task is hard not because its
components conflict but because the Plus component lies outside what the
organisms' plasticity can reach, pinning the best attainable fitness to the
threshold itself.

\subsection{Predictions versus observations}

quantity $F^* - \mathbb{E}[F(X)]$ -- large when acquired solutions are
heritable, accumulable, and reachable; zero when the ceiling cannot clear

\section{Discussion}

Our results provide the first quantitative test of Denis Noble's hypothesis
that organisms possess direct read--write access to their heredity -- and,
unusually for work in this area, the simulations come with a complete
analytical account. On learnable compositional problems, \Pheno{} achieved
3.0--6.0$\times$ higher fitness than the Baldwin Effect ($p < 0.001$,
Cohen's $d = 3.44$--$4.89$), and the analysis shows exactly why: write-back
converts learning into a heritable ratchet that climbs to the ceiling of what
plasticity can reach, while learning without inheritance has zero
heritability and therefore, by the breeder's equation, zero expected response
to selection.

\subsection{Direct write-back versus selection-based agency}

The Extended Evolutionary Synthesis recognises that organisms actively shape
evolution through niche construction, developmental plasticity, and learning.
Yet EES remains fundamentally selection-based: learning influences the
phenotype, which alters selection pressure, which eventually reshapes genes
over many generations. Noble's proposal is different in kind: organisms do
not merely influence evolution through selection, they write learned patterns
directly into heritable form. Our model quantifies that distinction
sharply. With the learning operator held literally identical between the two
algorithms, the entire 3--6$\times$ gap is attributable to what happens to
acquired information at reproduction. Three regimes emerge:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simple tasks} ($1.0\times$): inheritance is irrelevant when a
        single pattern can be learned within one lifetime.
  \item \textbf{Learnable compositional tasks} ($3$--$6\times$): the
        write-back ratchet accumulates components across generations;
        offspring inherit parents' solutions and learning adds to them. The
        advantage equals the computable gap $F^* - \mathbb{E}[F(X)]$ between
        the reachability ceiling and the neutral-drift baseline.
  \item \textbf{Unreachable compositional tasks} ($1.0\times$): when no
        reachable learned state clears the write-back threshold, the ratchet
        never engages and write-back degenerates to selection-only learning.
\end{itemize}

The analysis also dissolves a natural objection. One might worry that
\Pheno{}'s advantage reflects extra heritable \emph{capacity} (genome plus
pattern list) rather than write-back as such. Proposition~\ref{prop:baldwin}
shows the real driver is heritability, not capacity: Baldwin's failure is
not that it stores too little, but that nothing it learns is transmitted at
all -- its heritable line drifts neutrally regardless of how much capacity it
carries.

\subsection{Limits of extended agency: reachability and the write threshold}

The L+Plus result sets a principled boundary on extended agency, and the
analysis corrects the interpretation we ourselves might otherwise have given
it. The two patterns do not conflict on the grid. Rather, the Plus target
lies almost entirely outside the set of cells the organisms' plasticity can
express, which pins the best attainable fitness at exactly the write-back
threshold -- so nothing is ever deemed worth writing. The general lesson is
that epigenomic write-back cannot rescue what plasticity cannot reach:
organismal agency over heredity is bounded by the expressive range of the
phenotype-generating machinery, and by the stringency of the write
criterion. Both bounds have biological analogues -- developmental constraint
on what variation is expressible \citep{WestEberhard2003}, and the
selectivity of which physiological states become stably marked in the
epigenome \citep{Jablonka2014}.

\subsection{Biological plausibility and scope}

Real epigenetic inheritance (DNA methylation, histone modifications)
persists through meiosis and transmits acquired information across
generations \citep{Pembrey2006,WaterlandJirtle2007}. Our model idealises
this channel: perfect copying fidelity, a strict write threshold, and binary
patterns. The core principle -- learned regulatory changes inherited with
near-complete fidelity and accumulated across generations -- is biologically
grounded, and the model's compositional structure mirrors how major
transitions layer solutions: compartmentalisation before nuclear transport,
adhesion before differentiation \citep{MaynardSmith1995}. Inherited
accumulation lets a lineage secure one layer and then search for the next,
avoiding the exponential cost of rediscovering everything each generation.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}

We tested artificial grid tasks, not ecological complexity; binary patterns,
not continuous traits; perfect epigenomic fidelity, not realistic decay; and
asexual lineages, not sexual recombination. The analytical account, however,
suggests exactly where these relaxations will bite: epigenomic decay turns
the ratchet into a leaky ratchet whose equilibrium should sit below $F^*$ by
a computable amount; recombination mixes pattern lists between lineages; and
genetically guided (rather than genome-independent) learning would
reintroduce a Baldwinian channel, allowing genetic assimilation to compete
with write-back on even terms. Each is a concrete, quantitative follow-up
within the same enumerable framework. A companion manuscript develops the
analytical results in full generality, including waiting-time distributions
for ratchet engagement and finite-horizon corrections to the ceiling.

\section{Conclusion}

We demonstrated that heritable epigenomic write-back of learned patterns
accelerates evolutionary problem-solving 3--6 fold on compositional tasks,
and we explained the result exactly: write-back turns lifetime learning into
a monotone ratchet that converges to the ceiling of what plasticity can
reach, whereas learning without inheritance has zero heritability and leaves
the heritable line drifting neutrally. The advantage is therefore specific
and computable -- zero on tasks solvable within one lifetime, zero again when
no reachable state clears the write criterion, and equal to the gap between
the reachability ceiling and the neutral baseline in between. This
specificity is what makes the result mechanistic rather than merely
philosophical: organisms that write what they learn into heredity can solve
compositional problems their selection-only counterparts provably cannot.
